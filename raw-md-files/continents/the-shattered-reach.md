# The Shattered Reach

**Vhonoris** (the old continent, now used only historically), **the Shattered Reach** or simply **the Reach**. Formerly a single landmass, now a scattered archipelago of several thousand islands, skerries, and half-drowned peaks in the world's southeastern sea.

---

### Geological Background, Before

Vhonoris was, for centuries, the world's preeminent seat of magical scholarship. Its bedrock sat atop an unusually dense confluence of ley-lines (six major convergences by the old cartographers' count, more than any other landmass) which made it both the most magically fertile soil in the world and, in hindsight, the most dangerous place to build a civilization. Its cities were raised on and around these convergences the way other nations build around rivers: **Cael Vhon**, the capital, sat directly atop the largest confluence so potent that ordinary stone buildings there needed no mortar, only correctly-inscribed foundation glyphs, to hold themselves together.

The continent was fertile, temperate, mountainous in its spine and lush along its coasts, and it exported magical expertise the way other nations export grain: enchanted goods, trained battle-mages, alchemical reagents, and scholars-for-hire were Vhonoris's primary trade goods for the better part of four centuries. Its universities were considered the finest anywhere, and a Vhonic education was, for a time, the single most valuable credential a mage could hold.

What remains of Vhonoris is referred to only by its aftermath.

---

## 1. The Cataclysm

### The Sundering

Roughly three generations ago (dates are disputed; the Reach's own record-keeping did not survive the event that destroyed it), the continent's mage-councils attempted a working now referred to, euphemistically, as **the Binding**, a continent-spanning ritual intended to permanently lock all six ley-convergences together into a single stable network, in theory eliminating the periodic "wild seasons" of magical instability that had plagued Vhonoris for as long as anyone could remember, and in practice granting whichever council controlled the resulting network an almost unanswerable degree of magical leverage over the others. Accounts differ on whether it was reckless ambition, sabotage by a rival council, or simple miscalculation of scale, and the Reach's surviving peoples tend to regard the question as beside the point.

What is agreed upon: the working detonated the convergences.

The event is called, without much elaboration needed, **the Sundering**. In the space of a single night, the six convergence-points erupted more or less simultaneously, tearing the continent apart along the fault-lines the ley-network had been built to bind. Vhonoris did not sink so much as it was *shredded*. Entire mountain ranges thrown into the sea as boulder-fields, whole cities dropped intact onto the ocean floor still standing, and the remaining fragments of solid ground scattered across an area of open water several times the size of the original continent.

Nothing that survives from before the Sundering treats it as anything but the defining fact of the world it left behind. The peoples of the Reach date things from it the way other cultures date things from a founding or a flood.

### The Mist and the Wild Water

The Sundering left the surrounding sea saturated with unspent magical residue, which the local peoples call **the Wild Water**. Currents in the Reach behave unpredictably around old convergence-sites, occasionally running against wind and tide for no discernible reason; a permanent low haze, **the Mist**, clings to certain stretches of open water and is known to disorient navigation instruments that rely on anything more sophisticated than a compass and a steady hand. Sailors who cross into a thick bank of Mist without a local pilot are, at best, delayed for days.

The Wild Water is not uniformly dangerous, in fact, most of the inhabited Reach sits in comparatively calm water, but it means that "chart" is a loose word here. Channels shift by season. A route that was safe last year's monsoon may be silted, reef-choked, or simply *wrong* by this year's. Local pilotage in the Reach is the single most valuable skill a person can sell, and entire livelihoods are built on knowing forty miles of water intimately.

## 2. Geography Today

### The Archipelago

What was once a single continent is now conventionally divided by local sailors into three loose zones, moving outward from the old continental core:

| Zone | Description |
|---|---|
| **The Hearth-Shards** | The largest surviving fragments, some still tens of miles across, generally clustered near where the continent's mountainous spine once stood. Mountainous, rocky, least habitable, but source of most stone and metal. |
| **The Reach proper** | Thousands of small-to-mid islands, reefs, and sandbars consisting of the shattered coastal lowlands and farmland of old Vhonoris, now the archipelago's main population belt. |
| **The Drowned Marches** | The outermost ring, mostly submerged, where only rooftops, spires, and the tops of old convergence-towers still break the surface at low tide. Considered dangerous, only half-legal to salvage, and thick with Mist. |

Land in the Reach proper is characteristically small, low, and split by narrow channels rather than open coastline. The region resembles a river delta more than a normal archipelago, because in a sense it is one: much of it is old farmland and floodplain that dropped only a few feet rather than being obliterated outright, leaving a maze of shallow, brackish waterways between islands that are sometimes only a rice-paddy's width apart. Locals frequently move between islands not by open sea but by threading these inner channels, which are calmer, more predictable, and largely free of the Wild Water's worse effects.

### Climate

The Reach sits in a warm, wet band, monsoon-driven, with a pronounced wet season in which the inner channels swell and connect islands that are dry-season-separate, and a dry season in which the same channels shrink back down, sometimes to mudflats passable on foot. Settlements plan their entire yearly rhythm, like building, market cycles, travel, even marriage timing in some communities, around this flood-and-recede pattern, since a route or a market square that exists in the dry season may be three feet underwater by the wet.

Heat is heavy and constant; storms are frequent and, closer to the old convergence-sites, occasionally accompanied by visible discharges of stray magical energy in the clouds themselves, a phenomenon locals call **glass-lightning** for the way it leaves fused, glossy scars in whatever it strikes.

### Land Scarcity and the Life Aquatic

Solid, reliably dry land is the Reach's scarcest resource, scarcer than food, scarcer than fresh water, scarcer even than lumber. This single fact shapes nearly everything about how the region is inhabited: the population overwhelmingly lives *on* the water. Houseboats, raft-clusters, and stilt-built platforms driven into shallow silt are more common than any structure built on bedrock. A family's boat is frequently its house, its storeroom, its workshop, and, when lashed to a dozen others at anchor, its neighborhood, all at once. Owning a patch of solid, storm-safe ground is a marker of real wealth in a way that owning a large boat is not.

## 3. The Floating Markets

The single most recognizable institution of the Reach, and the clearest expression of its land-scarce, water-bound way of life, is the **floating market**, or in the local trade-pidgin, a **raft-street**.

A raft-street begins, functionally, as an anchorage: a stretch of calm inner channel or sheltered bay where boats habitually gather to trade. Over time, semi-permanent rafts, flat, lashed platforms built specifically to sell from rather than to sail, are added and tied off to one another and to fixed anchor-piles driven into the channel bed, until what exists is not a collection of individual boats so much as a single sprawling, floating street grid that can run for as long as quarter-mile of interconnected platforms, walkways, and gangplanks. Vendors set up permanent or semi-permanent stalls directly on these raft-platforms; buyers walk, rather than paddle, from stall to stall, stepping across narrow plank-bridges between rafts the way one would cross a street. Smaller trading craft (canoes, coracles, flat-bottomed skiffs) thread the gaps between the larger rafts and platforms, selling directly out of the boat to people standing on the rafts above.

Raft-streets are rebuilt, extended, or partially dismantled with the seasons. A large raft-street at the height of dry season, when channels are calm and shallow, may shrink to a fraction of its size once the wet-season floods raise the water and scatter the anchorage, only to be re-lashed and re-expanded once the water settles again. A given raft-street's exact footprint is therefore never quite fixed twice; regulars navigate by rough neighborhood ("the dye-rafts," "the net-market," "the outer ring where the Hearth-Shard traders tie up") rather than by any fixed address, since the physical location of a given stall may shift a few dozen yards from one wet season to the next.

Specific raft-streets tend to specialize, both by custom and by simple practicality. A fish-and-produce raft-street wants to be near the outer channel where boats can offload quickly and where the smell doesn't linger over the residential rafts; a salvage-and-scrap raft-street, dealing in material pulled up from the Drowned Marches, tends to sit further out, partly for space and partly because buyers of Marches-salvage prefer not to be seen too closely associating with it. The largest raft-streets, at the mouths of the busiest channels, function as the closest thing the Reach has to a true city center: dense, loud, semi-permanent, and layered three and four rafts deep in places, with narrow raised walkways strung between anchor-poles above the stalls themselves so that porters and pole-runners can move goods overhead without wading through the crowd below.

Trade at a raft-street is conducted almost entirely from the boat or raft itself. A seller rarely, if ever, sets foot on dry land in the course of a normal trading day, and prices, disputes, and reputations travel by word of mouth along the channel network faster than any written record could, and a raft-street vendor who cheats a buyer one morning will have every neighboring raft aware of it by the evening tide.

## 4. Settlement Types

Beyond the raft-streets themselves, the Reach's inhabited geography sorts roughly into four settlement forms, often overlapping within a single community:

- **Anchor-clusters**: groups of independently owned houseboats that moor together, sometimes permanently lashed, sometimes simply anchored in loose proximity out of habit and mutual protection. The most common form of settlement by sheer population.
- **Stilt-towns**: fixed platforms driven into shallow silt or a reef shelf, built up over generations into wooden townscapes on legs, tall enough to clear the wet-season flood line. Considered a step up in permanence and status from an anchor-cluster, though still fundamentally water-built.
- **Shard-holds**: settlements built on actual solid rock, almost exclusively found on the Hearth-Shards. Rare, defensible, and disproportionately wealthy and politically influential relative to their small population.
- **March-camps**: seasonal, semi-legal salvage camps set up in the Drowned Marches during low-Mist stretches, dismantled and moved before the Mist thickens again. Considered dangerous, lucrative, and slightly disreputable work.

## 5. Resources and Economy

The Reach's economy runs, out of necessity, on what the water provides and what can be pulled up from what the water swallowed.

- **Fish, shellfish, and channel-farmed rice** form the basic subsistence economy nearly everywhere solid, brackish-safe farmland exists, typically on the calmer inner islands of the Reach proper, planted in flooded paddies that double as fish-ponds in the wet season.
- **Salvage** from the Drowned Marches is the Reach's most distinctive export: worked stone, enchanted fixtures, intact glassware, and (most valuably) inert magical components from before the Sundering, pulled up by divers and salvage-crews willing to work in Mist-heavy water. Salvage goods are graded and sold with a heavy premium for anything provably pre-Sundering, both for their craftsmanship and for the (usually correct) assumption that they were made by mages whose skill hasn't been matched since.
- **Dye and lacquer work**, produced from a handful of hardy coastal and shard-growing plants, is common enough to overlap somewhat with the Reach's neighboring trade partners, though Reach lacquerware, waterproofed by necessity, often decorated with patterns derived from channel-maps — is distinctive enough to be recognized on sight in foreign ports.
- **Pilotage**, as noted above, is sold as a service in its own right: local pilots who know a given stretch of channel or Mist-bank are hired by outside traders for single crossings, and a reputation as a reliable pilot is one of the few forms of individual wealth-building available to someone without land, boat-fleet, or salvage rights.
