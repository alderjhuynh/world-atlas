# Medicinal Flora

No single continent's herbcraft resembles another's, for the same reason nothing else about them does: each tradition grew up treating the specific damage its own local magic does to a body. A Kyrai healer, a Vaelmourn apothecary, and a Witchlands *saelea* would each recognize "medicine" as a category, and would each, handed the other's remedy chest, have almost no idea what half of it was for.

---

## 1. Emberfern (Kyre)

**Emberfern**, called in Kyrai simply *tovash-la*, is a broad-fronded understory fern found almost nowhere but the shaded creek-cuts of the Kyresh foothills, within a few days' walk of an exposed kyrite seam and, pointedly, nowhere the foreign concessions have ever bothered to survey for it. It grows best in soil with faint residual kyrite dust worked into it, the same soil miners' boots track home every evening, which Kyrai herbalists take as more than coincidence: a plant that only thrives on the ground that causes stonelung, and that treats stonelung's first symptoms better than anything else known, is spoken of among healers less as a remedy than as a kind of standing debt the forest pays back on its own.

Dried emberfern fronds, steeped into a bitter, rust-colored tea, do not cure stonelung, no Kyrai healer claims otherwise, but a cup taken daily measurably slows the deadening in the extremities that marks the condition's early stage, and a course of it taken consistently over a season can, in documented cases, restore some feeling to fingers and toes already gone half-numb. It does nothing at all for advanced cases where the numbness has begun reaching a person's emotional responsiveness; interior healers are blunt about this limit.

Emberfern's real weight, though, is political rather than medical. Because it only grows wild in the free interior and cannot be dried and stored for more than a season without losing its potency, it cannot be stockpiled by anyone trying to run mine labor past the point their body can take, only smuggled fresh, a few fronds at a time, along the same border margin the resistance already uses to move people and kinstones. A concession overseer who notices a miner's hands are staying steadier than they should be is, more often than not, looking at evidence of a supply line he has no way to trace back through, since fresh emberfern carries no scent or mark that any assayer has ever learned to test for.

## 2. Quickmoss (Vaelmourn)

Deep in the blind, sunless fungal groves that the marrow-caverns' own residual warmth sustains, threaded low along the cavern floor where marrow-miners' lamplight rarely lingers long enough to notice it, grows **quickmoss**: a pale, faintly warm moss that Vaelmourn apothecaries rate as the single most useful remedy for the Hollow that doesn't require a Choir attestation to buy.

Quickmoss is harvested by the same guilds that work the marrow-caverns, typically by junior renderers sent down with the haul-crews specifically to strip a season's growth before it's trampled underfoot by people who don't yet know to look for it. Rendered into a thin, faintly bitter paste and taken under the tongue, it does not reverse the Hollow, nothing on Vaelmourn does, but it reliably eases the earliest stage, the Chill, and can measurably slow a vein-tender's slide from Chill into the longer-lasting Numbing if taken consistently through a heavy rotation. Choir physicians prescribe it routinely to anyone working an active vein and consider it, in the Choir's own dry phrasing, "the difference between a season's discomfort and a season's cost."

What quickmoss cannot do, and what no apothecary on the continent has ever managed to make it do, is touch the Deep Hollow once it sets in. Physicians are candid about this with patients rather than letting the moss's reputation as a general remedy overpromise: past a certain point, quickmoss is a comfort rather than a treatment, and continuing to rely on it as though it were still working is, itself, treated as one of the quieter warning signs that a physician or vein-tender has stopped being able to tell how far along they already are.

## 3. Saelroot (the Witchlands)

**Saelroot**, or in Aurathil simply *sael-tor*, "the growth-bound one," is a deep-taprooted grass cousin found across the Coastal Fringe and the wetter stretches of the Open Country, thriving in exactly the stiller, near-ground air that growth-work itself draws on. It is, unglamorously, one of the "dried herbs and reagents unique to the grass biome" that make up the bulk of what little the Witchlands exports, and every coven of any size keeps a working store of it dried and bundled, since it is, by a wide margin, the standard remedy for windburn.

Chewed raw or steeped into a strong, faintly sweet infusion, saelroot eases exactly the symptoms windburn produces: it soothes chapped lips and a raw throat within a day, and a course of the tea taken over two or three days noticeably shortens the ache behind the sternum that follows a hard casting. Apprentices are given it as a matter of course after their first few overreaches, sometimes before they've even admitted to a teacher that they pushed too hard, since a young caster reaching for the saelroot bundle unprompted is, itself, a fairly reliable tell.

Saelroot does nothing for wind-fallen, and covens are careful never to imply otherwise to a grieving apprentice; the loss of a trustworthy breathy *-kho* is a matter of standing, not sensation, and no herb touches standing. It is, likewise, no use at all against storm-debt, which every coven elder will say, usually without much elaboration, is not the kind of thing a root is for. What saelroot is good for, and the reason growth-workers cultivate it deliberately near a coven's rooted camps rather than relying on the wild stock alone, is the ordinary, unglamorous cost of ordinary practice: it is the reason a coven's young storm-casters can afford to overreach fairly often in training without it costing them a season's voice each time.

## 4. Marshwick (the Shattered Reach)

**Marshwick** is a hollow-stemmed reed that grows thick along the channel-farmed paddies of the Reach proper, tolerant of brackish water and, notably, of the faint Wild Water taint that sits in the soil near any old convergence-site closer than a few miles, which ordinary rice will not tolerate at all. Rohai farmers have always planted a marshwick border around a paddy that sits too near the Mist for comfort, less for the reed itself, which has no food value worth mentioning, than for what it tells them: marshwick that grows pale and stunted is read as a plain, physical warning that the water nearby is carrying more residue than a season's crop should be grown in.

Chewed fresh, the stem's bitter inner pith is the standard remedy for the disorientation of a thick Mist-crossing, taken by pilots and salvage-divers alike before working a heavy bank rather than after, since it blunts the worst of the nausea and directional confusion rather than reversing it once it's set in. Quieting-men chew it constantly during a long session of draining a recovered item, less for the Mist than for the low, persistent unease of sustained close contact with "live debt," and a quieting-man's stained teeth, marshwick's pith darkens them permanently after years of the habit, are a plain, unspoken mark of the trade, read on sight the way a Kyrai cutter's scarred hands would be.

It is not, and no Rohai herbalist has ever claimed it is, a cure for the deeper superstition marshwick brushes up against. A diver who goes missing in a bad Mist-bank and returns days later, "considerably changed" in the way the old accounts of failed Waygate crossings describe, is not helped by marshwick any more than by anything else, and offering someone a fresh stem in that circumstance is a kindness rather than a treatment, the same gesture as sitting with them rather than actually fixing anything.
