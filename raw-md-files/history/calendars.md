# Reckoning

There is no shared calendar anywhere in the world, and, unlike a shared language, no one has tried to build one. Common Language trade contracts get around this the unglamorous way: by naming a second, independently verifiable event, a market-day, a lunar phase, a season's first frost, alongside whatever date each party would use at home, so that a dispute over timing can be settled by pointing at the sky rather than at either side's calendar. Every people on this list would tell you that it is a poor substitute for reckoning done properly, which is to say, reckoning done their way.

---

## 1. The Vosarai: The Dye-Wheel

The Vosarai do not count years so much as *cycle* them, and the unit they cycle is the same aspectual sequence that structures a Vosarai life, a Vosarai verb, and a Vosarai garden: Budding, Blooming, Wilting, Dormant. A year, locally, is a **dye-wheel** (*le-orai*), and it is understood as the same four-stage motion the language already forces onto every other completed thing, applied this time to the turning of the seasons themselves rather than to a person, a trade, or a marriage.

### The Wheel's Twelve Turnings

Within a single dye-wheel, the calendar is subdivided not into abstract months but into **turnings**, each one named for whichever household cultivar is at its dye-ready peak that stretch of the year. A crescent household doesn't need to consult a written calendar to know roughly where in the wheel it stands; it needs only to look at which garden beds are currently worth cutting. The turnings run, in rough sequence from the wheel's Budding start:

| Turning | Named for | Rough character |
|---|---|---|
| *Oret-turn* | First-bud | The wheel's true beginning; the season of coming-of-age rites, since a child's own budding is timed, where possible, to align with the flower's |
| *Vanora-turn* | Bridge-lily | The traditional wedding season, bridge-lily grown in matched pairs coming into bloom together |
| *Mira-turn* | Honeyvein | Peak scent-training season for apprentice authenticators, when the flower's identifying resin is strongest |
| *Tomu-turn* | Duskbell | The long, steady inland turning; unremarkable by design, the wheel's equivalent of a workaday stretch |
| *Hosin-turn* | Saltbloom | The turning harbor-facing households mark most closely, since fresh saltbloom dye is the season's most legible proof of current goods |
| *Torai-turn* | Farbloom | The export turning, when the crescent's most jealously guarded cultivar is cut and the year's foreign trade is settled |
| *Kelun-turn* | Ashflower | The high-slope turning, cold and austere, when the year's harvest is assessed plainly, without much ceremony |
| *Oveta-turn* | Emberpetal | A short, urgent turning; guest-dye season, when the crescent expects and prepares for visitors before the cut blossoms brown |
| *Kenora-turn* | Rootbloom | The turning of remembrance; whatever deaths the year has held are formally rooted in this stretch, whether or not they occurred in it |
| *Hunora-turn* | Nightglass | The Honto's turning, when the stone-tenders read the cliffs and the wheel's fortune for the year ahead is taken from what they report |
| *Usera-turn* | Wilt-quiet | The mourning turning proper, when the communal grey-green flower is worn by whichever households are still counting a dye-season of grief |
| *Shara-turn* | Harborthorn | The wheel's closing turning, restless and faintly disreputable, before the cycle folds back into Oret-turn |

A given household's own year, notably, does not have to run turning-to-turning in lockstep with its neighbors'. A family in mourning is living inside its own private Wilting stretch regardless of which turning the rest of the crescent is currently in, the same flexibility Vosarai custom already extends to an individual's life-stage. The dye-wheel is a shared frame everyone reads the same way; it is not a shared clock everyone is required to be on.

### Dating a Record

Because Vosarin grammar requires an evidential on every finite verb, a written or recited date is never a bare fact. A contract dated with the **witnessed** suffix asserts that the scribe personally saw the turning named take place, the flower actually cut, the rite actually performed. A date given with the **reported** suffix is understood to be secondhand and, in any serious dispute, worth less. A date given with the **inferred** suffix, "some time in Tomu-turn, by the state of the dye stock on hand", is common enough in casual household record-keeping but would be laughed out of a weave-hall contract. The market arbiter's power to re-mark a trader's claimed evidential, described elsewhere, applies with particular force to dates: a merchant who backdates a contract, claiming witnessed knowledge of a turning they couldn't possibly have observed, is committing a specific, well-understood, and severely punished kind of fraud.

## 2. The Rohai: The Long Count and the Tide-Notch

The Driftfolk of the Shattered Reach share, with total uniformity across every fleet and raft-street, exactly one fixed epoch: the Sundering. Every formal reckoning of years runs forward from it, written or spoken as **AS**, *After the Sundering*, and nothing before it is dated at all in the ordinary sense, since nothing before it survived in a form anyone still living can verify. Asking a Rohai elder what happened in a given pre-Sundering year is understood as roughly the same category of question as asking what a debt looked like before anyone alive was born to owe it: technically a real thing, functionally unanswerable, and not really the point.

### Why There Is No Rohai Month

There is no continent-wide Rohai calendar finer than the year, and this is not an oversight; it follows the same logic that keeps the Reach without a government. A fixed monthly calendar would need to be issued and enforced by somebody, and the Rohai trust arithmetic and witnessed record over any single issuing authority for exactly the reason they trust a split token over a spoken promise. What exists instead is **the tide-notch**: a length of cord or a notched stick, kept by any household, boat, or fleet that needs one, marking days elapsed since some locally agreed starting point, usually the signing of a trade agreement or the start of a voyage, rather than since any fixed calendar date at all. Two fleets settling a contract don't ask what month it is; they ask how many notches have passed since the goods changed hands, and the notched stick itself, like the split trade-token it resembles, is often physically divided between the two parties so that neither can quietly add or shave a day without it showing.

Longer-range dating, the kind that matters for inheritance, old debts, or fleet history, is kept the way everything else important is kept among the Rohai: recited, not written. The **Long Tally**'s tally-singers don't only recite who is owed what; they recite *when*, anchoring events to the year-count AS and, within a year, to the season, since the Reach's monsoon-driven wet and dry seasons are reliable enough, and universal enough across the archipelago, to serve as the one subdivision every Rohai agrees on without needing an institution to declare it. A debt is dated to "the third dry season after the Sundering's two-hundred-and-eleventh year," not to any month, and a tally-singer challenged mid-recitation on a date is expected to be able to defend that placement against the Tally's other entries the same way they'd defend any other detail.

### The Unnamed Years

One deliberate gap in the Long Count is worth noting: a year in which the Grudge, the storm-echo of the Sundering's debt, did unusual damage is never dated by the storm itself, in keeping with the broader taboo against naming storms and thereby inviting them back into standing account. Instead such a year is recorded in the Tally by what it cost: "the year the Kestrel-fleet lost its keel-masters," or similar, tying the date permanently to a loss rather than to a weather event, so that the storm itself never receives the small, ongoing social reality that a name would give it.

## 3. The Wind-Count

Outsiders arriving in the Witchlands looking for a calendar run into the same wall they run into looking for a throne. There has never been one, and the reasoning locals give is the identical, well-worn line: *the grass doesn't hold a border, and it doesn't hold a date either.* A coven's sense of "when" is built the same way its sense of "where" is built, out of accumulated, personally verified memory rather than a fixed external record, and the two are not treated as separate skills.

### Aspect Applied to the Year

What Aurathil does have, and applies to timekeeping the same way it applies to a working or a storm, is its four-part aspectual cycle: Rising, Gusting, Falling, Still. A coven speaks of the year's broad shape in exactly these terms rather than in named months: the wet season is spoken of as the year **Rising**, building toward the peak of growth and travel; the height of the dry season's heat and hardest grazing is the year **Gusting**, at its most demanding; the cooling stretch as the grass burns gold and thins is the year **Falling**; and the short, coldest lull before growth resumes is the year **Still**. This is not a fixed calendar so much as a shared vocabulary for describing where a year currently stands, useful for a conversation between two covens but useless for pinning an exact date, since one coven's Rising and another's, a hundred miles and a different weather-front away, may be weeks apart without either being wrong.

### Dating by Memory and by Epithet

Absent a fixed calendar, Witchlands events are dated the way Witchlands directions are given: relative to something specific and locally verified, rather than to an abstract, universal count. "The season *Auraea* turned back the Gale" or "two winds before the coven split at the Standing Wood" are entirely normal, entirely sufficient ways to place an event in time, and a coven's collective memory of its own epithet-bearers functions, informally, as a kind of chronology, since a caster's rise to and eventual loss of an epithet like *auraea* or *silea* is exactly the sort of well-witnessed, widely retold event a whole region can use as a shared marker for years without anyone needing to write it down.

### The Waystation Stones

The one place anything like a shared, cross-coven calendar exists at all is at a **waystation**, precisely because a waystation belongs to no one and is trusted by everyone. Several of the larger, longer-standing waystations maintain a **year-stone**: a plain marker where any coven passing through may cut a small, agreed notch to record that they were there, alongside, where the custom is kept, a rough count of winds since the stone's first notch. It is the closest thing to a fixed epoch the Witchlands has ever produced, and it works only because it belongs to the same neutral, no-one's-territory logic that keeps waystations themselves outside any coven's authority. A caster who tried to notch a year-stone with a false date, backdating a claim the way a Vosarai trader might, would find the offense treated with the same gravity as violating waystation neutrality in any other form, which is to say, among the very few things the Witchlands punishes close to universally.

## 4. Vaelmourn: Shroud-Reckoning

Vaelmourn's inhabitants keep, in practice, two calendars at once, one for the Ribcage basin below and one for the rib-tops above.

### The Basin Calendar

Basin-folk, who farm and trade on comparatively stable, Held ground, keep time the way most settled agricultural peoples do: by planting and harvest cycles, tracked informally against the marrow-caverns' own steady, weather-independent output, since marrow-mining, unlike farming, runs year-round and gives the basin a reliable, always-available point of reference regardless of season. Basin years are counted loosely from whichever Choir-recognized event a given hold considers significant, most commonly the founding of that hold's oldest shrine, and a basin-dweller asked what year it is will typically answer with their own hold's count rather than any single continent-wide figure, then add, without much concern, that a neighboring hold three valleys over would give a different number for the same year.

### The Rib Calendar and the Fire-Glass

Rib-top settlements keep a stricter, more formally marked calendar, because so much of Serevain daily life, tier movement, span maintenance, true-setter certification, depends on being able to say precisely how long ago something was confirmed sound. A true-setter's mark is valid for exactly one year and re-tested annually; a Span is inspected on a fixed schedule; and the **fire-glass**, the seasonal recitation of a rib's Climbed dead, is held once a season without exception, giving every rib-top settlement four fixed, unmissable calendar anchors a year regardless of whatever else is or isn't formally dated. Between fire-glasses, ordinary rib time is read less by calendar than by the **Shroud** itself: shroud-readers, trained over years to track how the permanent cloud cover at height thickens, thins, and rarely, briefly, parts, treat those changes as a seasonal signal, consulted before any undertaking large enough to need a good omen, in much the way a lowland farmer might consult the sky before planting.

## 5. Kyre: Two Calendars, Kept On Purpose

Nowhere in the world is the gap between calendars more openly, deliberately political than on Kyre, where the interior's reckoning and the coast's imposed one were never meant to be reconciled at all.

### The Lineage Round

Kyrai lineages in the free interior keep no single continent-wide calendar, any more than they keep a single continent-wide government, and for the same reason. Each lineage marks its year by its own **seasonal round**: the cycle of hunting, gathering, and small-scale kyrite-working that has structured Kyrai forest life since long before the first foreign ship reached the coast. Wolf-, deer-, hawk-, and river-otter-lineage communities each date their year, in large part, by the migratory and breeding patterns of their own namesake animal, so that a hawk-lineage year and a river-otter-lineage year, tracking two different creatures on two different rhythms, simply don't line up, and no Kyrai finds this remarkable any more than they'd find it remarkable that two lineages speak with slightly different accents. A finer, shared marker exists in the **seam-tide**: exposed kyrite deposits are known locally to pulse faintly brighter on a slow, roughly seasonal cycle of their own, tied, by most interior accounts, to the same deep rhythm the ancestor-forest itself keeps, and a Kinworker timing a difficult carving to the seam-tide's brightest stretch is timing it the way a farmer elsewhere might time a planting to the moon.

### The Concession Calendar

Every occupied stretch of coast runs, instead, on whichever foreign power's home calendar its administration imports wholesale, fixed months, fixed weeks, fixed quotas, applied to the mines and plantations with no reference to the interior's seasonal round or the seam-tide at all. This is not incidental. Quota schedules, shift rotations, and shipping dates are all set by the concession's own distant capital, and the imposition of a foreign calendar onto Kyrai labor is, in the driest administrative sense, simply another extraction: a schedule built entirely around when the concession wants stone out of the ground and onto a ship, with no consideration for anything the land or its people might otherwise be marking that same stretch of time for.

Enslaved Kyrai artisans and laborers are required to work the concession calendar and continue, quietly and at real risk, to track their own lineage round alongside it, a second, private reckoning kept the same way lineage identity and ancestor-practice are kept generally: unspoken where it needs to be, remembered regardless. The resistance's own coordination deliberately exploits the gap between the two systems. A raid or a supply run timed to the seam-tide or a lineage's own seasonal marker, rather than to any date a concession's shipping schedule would recognize as significant, is, by design, close to invisible to an administration that has never had reason to learn the interior's calendar and has generally assumed, with the same confidence it once assumed the continent had no real civilization, that none worth learning exists.
