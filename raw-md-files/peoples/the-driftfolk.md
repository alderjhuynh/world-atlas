# The Driftfolk of the Shattered Reach

**Rohai** (the people's own name for themselves, roughly "those who reckon" or "the balanced"), **the Driftfolk** in Common Language. A boat-dwelling, salvage-economy people scattered across the islands, channels, and raft-streets of the Shattered Reach, descended from survivors of the Sundering who were left, in the space of one night, with no surviving government, no surviving records, and almost no way to tell who could be trusted. Nearly everything distinctive about Rohai culture can be traced back to that single fact: a society that had to rebuild trust from nothing, using only what could be counted, split, and physically checked.

---

## 1. Cosmology and the Ledger-Logic

Rohai belief is organized around **owed** and **owing**, or the standing question of what is currently held against a person, and what a person currently holds against others. This is not treated as a purely economic idea; it extends to guilt, gratitude, grief, and the gods alike. A rescued sailor owes a debt to whomever pulled them from the water, and that debt is spoken of using the same vocabulary, and often the same physical tokens, as an unpaid trade contract. A community that survives a bad storm season together is said to be "balanced" with one another for a full year afterward, obligated to mutual aid without needing to ask.

This logic reaches all the way back to the Sundering itself, which most Rohai regard not as a natural disaster but as a debt violently called in: the old mage-lords of Vhonoris are widely believed to have taken more from the world's underlying order than they ever intended to repay, and the Sundering was that account finally, catastrophically, settling itself. Magic, as a result, is treated as radioactive with unpaid debt. Rohai communities are deeply, practically wary of magic use for this reason, not out of superstition alone but because their entire moral vocabulary treats any exchange with an unclear price as the most dangerous kind there is.

### The Grudge

Storms in the Reach are widely believed to be echoes of the Sundering's unpaid debt made physical. Sailors call a bad storm **the Grudge**, and unlike some cultures' habit of naming storms as individual, almost sociable acquaintances, the Rohai deliberately avoid naming them at all, on the belief that naming a debt-echo gives it a kind of standing account with you personally, inviting it back. A storm is discussed afterward only in terms of what it took and from whom, tallied like any other loss.

### The Quieting-Men

Because so much of the Reach's most valuable salvage is inert pre-Sundering magic, and because ordinary Rohai will not knowingly buy or sell an item still carrying "live debt," every raft-street of any size retains at least one **quieting-man** (or -woman), a specialist trained to test a recovered item for lingering magical charge and, if necessary, perform the slow, unglamorous ritual work of draining or grounding it before it is considered safe to trade. Quieting-work is unglamorous, physically tedious, mildly dangerous, and paid extremely well; quieting-men occupy an odd social position, respected for a skill nobody else wants to learn and quietly avoided socially, since long-term proximity to live magical debt is widely, if unscientifically, believed to rub off.

Certain words, chiefly the formal titles once used by Vhonic mage-lords, are considered bad luck to say aloud at sea, on the belief that naming a defunct authority is itself a kind of debt claim on whatever ambient power still lingers in the water. Sailors who must refer to them at all use a set of indirect, agreed-upon substitutions instead.

## 2. Memory and the Long Tally

The Rohai keep no ancestor shrines and, generally, no household relics of the dead at all. An object can be lost, damaged, or disputed over, and a debt recorded in something breakable is a debt badly kept. Memory, instead, is kept the way any important account is kept among the Rohai: recited, publicly, from a living person's memory, in a fixed and testable form.

The **Long Tally** is an oral genealogical recitation, kept and extended by a trained specialist called a **tally-singer**. Every raft-street and larger anchor-cluster retains at least one. At funerals, name-days, and major disputes, the tally-singer recites the relevant portion of the community's dead as a functional record, since debts, obligations, and old disputes are frequently settled by appeal to what the Tally says a given ancestor did, owed, or was owed. A name still recited in the Tally is understood to still matter socially. A name that drops out because no tally-singer chooses to carry it forward, usually because nothing about the person's debts or obligations remains unresolved or relevant — is said to have **fallen out of the count**, and from that point the person is remembered only as part of the undifferentiated dead, no longer an individual account.

Tally-singers train for years and are tested publicly and often adversarially: a rival singer, or simply a skeptical elder, may challenge a recitation mid-performance, demanding the singer justify a detail against a second source. A tally-singer caught inventing or embellishing a debt-record loses standing immediately and, in serious cases, is barred from performing at all, since a false entry in the Tally is, functionally, a false debt claim against a real family.

## 3. Craft-Rank and Belonging

Rohai status is organized far more by demonstrated skill than by age. A person's standing in the community is expressed through a **rank**, earned through direct, witnessed accomplishment rather than through a ceremony tied to a life-stage, and a person can hold different ranks in different crafts simultaneously. A master pilot might still be a green-hand at salvage diving, and would be addressed accordingly depending on which capacity they're acting in.

| Rank | How it's earned | General standing |
|---|---|---|
| **Green-hand** | Default rank for anyone untested in a given craft, regardless of age | Cannot contract independently in that craft; works under a Line-hand's name |
| **Line-hand** | Completion of a formally witnessed first solo task | Can contract independently; testimony counts at full weight in disputes |
| **Keel-master** | Sustained, community-recognized excellence over years, confirmed by a vote of standing Line-hands and Keel-masters in the same craft | Can take on apprentices; disputes involving their craft are often referred to them informally before reaching a Split (see below) |
| **Fleet-elder** | Retirement from active practice after Keel-master standing, by choice or incapacity | No longer contracts directly, but carries the highest weight in any dispute requiring judgment over experience rather than fresh skill |

Advancement is not smooth or guaranteed, and a serious, provable failure can formally demote a person back to green-hand in that craft, regardless of prior standing, following a public review. This is rare, deeply shaming, and taken with total seriousness; a demoted Keel-master is spoken of, gently but unmistakably, as someone who has "gone back into debt."

Completed dangerous dives, crossings, or trade seasons are recorded as small, deliberately plain notches cut into a person's own mast or, for those without a boat of their own, tattooed along the forearm.

## 4. Kinship and the Fleet

Because the Sundering killed enormous numbers of people all at once and scattered survivors without regard to family, Rohai kinship was substantially reconstituted in the generations after the catastrophe around **fleets** — working convoys of boats that travel, salvage, and trade together. A fleet typically does include multiple blood-related families, but adoption into a fleet, formalized by a witnessed oath and a shared entry in that fleet's section of the Long Tally, carries essentially equal social weight to being born into one, and the distinction is rarely mentioned in daily life.

Marriage among the Rohai is enacted through a single, deliberately irreversible economic act: a coin, usually a plain, unminted disc of hammered metal made specifically for the purpose, is split unevenly in half with a chisel, and each partner keeps one half. The uneven split is the point: neither half can be mistaken for a matching whole from a different pair, which prevents fraud, and the two halves can be visually rejoined at any later date to publicly confirm the marriage still stands. Children born into the marriage are entered into both parents' fleet-lines in the Tally, formally connecting the two fleets' accounts to one another.

Divorce, is performed by returning both coin-halves to a smith and having them melted down together into a single, unremarkable lump, which is then donated to the raft-street's common fund rather than kept by either party.

## 5. Dress and the Debt-Cords

Rohai adornment is built around knotted cord-work rather than color or dye. A **debt-cord**, worn at the belt rather than hidden away, is a length of cord into which a person ties small, standardized knots recording specific outstanding debts and obligations, a literal, current personal ledger, worn visibly and expected to be shown without hesitation if a trade partner asks to check it. A Rohai adult with an empty debt-cord is not admired for it; an empty cord is mildly suspicious, read as someone who avoids the ordinary, healthy back-and-forth of mutual obligation that holds a fleet together, rather than as someone virtuously free of debt.

- **Rank-notches**, described above, mark craft accomplishment directly on mast or forearm.
- **Fleet-marks**, a small design element repeated on sail-patches and boat trim, identify which fleet a boat currently sails with; a boat that changes fleets has its fleet-mark visibly re-stitched, and an outdated fleet-mark left unchanged is considered both careless and slightly dishonest.
- **Mourning** is marked not by special dress but by a black-dyed cord tied temporarily alongside the debt-cord, recording the specific relationship to the deceased; it is removed, not at any fixed time, but at the point the mourner's own account with that person is privately felt to be settled.

## 6. Trade, Honesty, and the Split

Trust among the Rohai rests on visible, physical, hard-to-forge record-keeping rather than on any single office or on spoken oath alone. A significant trade agreement is typically sealed by physically splitting a marked token, tablet, or notched stick in two along an irregular, un-repeatable line, with each party keeping one half; the deal is only considered honored once the two halves are matched again at completion, at which point they're destroyed together, since a kept half after a deal's completion is itself a minor mark of suspicion.

When a dispute does arise that the parties can't settle privately, it's brought before **the Split**, a public hearing held in the open at a raft-street, not before a single judge but before a working panel of Keel-masters from crafts relevant to the dispute who hear both sides in front of a gathered crowd and render a joint ruling. A Split ruling is entered into the Long Tally by the raft-street's tally-singer and is considered binding precedent, cited by name in later disputes of a similar kind.

A trader caught deliberately falsifying a debt-cord or a split-token is subject to the harshest social penalty short of exile: a public, permanent notch cut into their own mast recording the specific fraud, impossible to remove without replacing the mast entirely, which most who receive one simply cannot afford to do.

## 7. Everyday Life

### The Watch Cycle

Rohai days are structured around nautical watches rather than tide-states or clock-hours, a habit retained from generations of families who slept, ate, and worked in rotating shifts aboard boats that never fully stopped moving even at anchor. Meals are eaten at watch-change rather than at fixed social hours, and because watches rotate through a household or fleet, it's entirely ordinary for different members of the same family to be eating their "morning" meal at different points in the actual day.

### Freshwater Rites

Because fresh water, is one of the Reach's tightest daily constraint for anyone living aboard rather than near a spring-fed island, rainfall collection is treated with a seriousness bordering on ceremony. A boat's rain-catch barrels are considered, in miniature, one of the few genuinely communal debts a fleet holds: sharing surplus fresh water after a good rain, or drawing on a neighbor's surplus after a dry stretch, is one of the most routine and least remarked-upon forms of the owed/owing relationship that runs through the rest of Rohai life, precisely because it happens so constantly that keeping strict account of it would be absurd, making it one of the very few debts the Rohai deliberately choose not to tally.

### Idiom

Everyday Rohai speech leans heavily on debt-and-ledger language even for situations with nothing to do with actual trade: a favor is "carried," a grudge is "an old notch," a person acting unusually generous is said to be "running a surplus," and a person who seems to be hiding something is described as someone "keeping a second cord", a private, undisclosed ledger, considered one of the worst things you can accuse a neighbor of.

## 8. Craft and the Guild-Fleets

Rohai craft-guilds are organized as fiercely separate, often quietly rival, **guild-fleets**. Pilots, divers, boatwrights, and quieting-men each keep their own fleet-line, their own section of the Long Tally, and their own rank-standards, and cross-training between them, while not forbidden, is unusual enough to draw comment. This separation is generally explained, when asked, in the same debt-logic that runs through the rest of Rohai life: each craft carries its own kind of risk and its own kind of debt to the people who practice it, and mixing the accounts too freely is considered a way of losing track of who really owes what to whom.

Diving guild-fleets, given the genuine danger of salvage work, maintain the strongest version of the mutual-aid custom known as **salt-debt**: an unspoken but absolutely binding obligation that any diver in trouble will be aided by whoever is nearest, regardless of guild-fleet rivalry, with the understanding that the debt this creates is settled later, in whatever form and at whatever pace the rescued diver's fleet can manage.

## 9. Death and the Sending

Death is treated, in Rohai custom, as the point at which a person's account is closed rather than passed on. When a Rohai dies, their debt-cord is cut from their body and burned separately, on the understanding that whatever they owed or were owed at the moment of death is now the fleet's collective responsibility to settle on their behalf, not something the dead carry forward.

The funeral itself, called **the Sending**, is a single rite rather than a sequence of stages: the body is laid on a small, unmanned raft built specifically for the purpose, set alight, and released on an outgoing tide to drift and burn out in open water, ideally toward the Drowned Marches rather than back through inhabited channels. Nothing is recovered afterward. This is deliberate and explicitly discussed as such: the Rohai do not want their dead physically returning to inhabited water even as ash, and the Sending is understood as full, clean release rather than transformation into some ongoing, tended presence.

The deceased's portion of the Long Tally is recited once, fully, at the Sending itself, and then only referenced afterward, never recited in full again.

## 10. Fleet-Sworn and Wake-Followers

Not every relationship among the Rohai carries fleet-level obligation. A clear, commonly used distinction separates the **fleet-sworn** (those formally entered into a fleet's line by blood, marriage-coin, or witnessed oath, and therefore bound by its full debts and covered by its salt-debt protections) from **wake-followers**: itinerant traders, hired hands, or harbor-folk who travel alongside a fleet for a season or a single long voyage without ever swearing into it.

Wake-followers are traded with honestly, protected from casual harm the same as anyone, and can build individual reputation and even rank within a specific craft over time, but they are explicitly, and without any implied insult, excluded from a fleet's salt-debt obligation and from entry in its section of the Tally. The Rohai regard this distinction as accuracy rather than coldness: a debt owed to everyone equally, they reason, stops meaning anything, and the entire structure of Rohai trust depends on being honest about exactly who is, and isn't, currently on the account.
